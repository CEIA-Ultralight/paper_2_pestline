Abstract.Industrial pest control is critical to food safety yet remains
largely manual, with inspectors visually counting insects in sticky traps.
This process is slow, expensive, non-scalable, and prone to high human
error. Automating this task via Computer Vision faces severe small ob-
ject detection challenges, such as the obliteration of morphological de-
tails due to feature vanishing, severe background clutter from industrial
debris mimicking biological targets, and specimen occlusion caused by
natural crowding on the adhesive surface. The literature presents two
competing approaches: model-centric methods enhancing architecture
and volume-centric methods expanding data through synthetic augmen-
tation. However, neither systematically evaluates the impact of capture
environment standardization on detection performance. This work pro-
posesadata-centricpipelineprioritizingphysicalcapturecontrol,dataset
curation, and ontological refinement. Using an identical YOLO architec-
ture across all experiments, we evaluate the evolution of the Sticky Trap
Insect Dataset family across its distinct curation stages. Results show
mAP@0.5 improvements from 0.476 to 0.608 on the smallest model vari-
ant (YOLO26-S) achieved exclusively through data engineering, provid-
ing robust empirical evidence that capture standardization and dataset
quality are the primary performance bottlenecks in microscale object
detection, fundamentally superseding architectural innovation.

1 Introduction
Pest control in food-processing industries is strictly regulated by frameworks like
ISO22000:2018[1]andHazardAnalysisandCriticalControlPoints(HACCP)[2].
2 M. P. C. M. Queiroz et al.
Compliance currently relies on the manual inspection of sticky traps [3], a slow,
non-scalable process with error rates up to 30% due to cognitive fatigue [4].
While Computer Vision offers an automated alternative, it faces severe Small
Object Detection (SOD) challenges because the targets are ultra-small. Primary
obstacles include “feature vanishing” during network downsampling [5], back-
ground clutter from adhesive residues that visually mimic insects, and specimen
occlusion caused by natural crowding on the traps.
The literature addresses SOD through three competing approaches. Model-
centric methods propose increasingly sophisticated architectures: lightweight
YOLO variants optimized for embedded inference [6], NMS-free training com-
bined with SPD-Conv modules to recover spatial information lost in strided con-
volutions [7], and specialized CNNs capable of simultaneously classifying species,
sex, and physiological status [8]. Volume-centric methods, in contrast, attempt to
compensate for data scarcity through synthetic image generation—typically via
3D rendering, or through aggressive augmentation pipelines. A third, inference-
centric line of work, exemplified by SAHI [9], intervenes neither on the training
data nor on the architecture, but on the inference pipeline itself, slicing high-
resolution images into overlapping patches to preserve small-object features that
would otherwise be lost during standard convolution. None of these three ap-
proaches, however, addresses the root cause of the problem: the procedure in
which training images are acquired. This gap motivates the central questions
driving our work:
RQ1:To what extent does dataset quality and physical capture stan-
dardization outperform increased model complexity in microscale insect
detection?
RQ2:Which component of a data-centric pipeline (ontological refine-
ment, physical capture standardization, or iterative curation) yields the
greatest individual performance gain?
RQ3:Can a low-complexity model, trained on a rigorously curated
dataset, achieve industrially acceptable detection performance without
architectural modification?
Our results establish the following contributions:
–A reproducible, three-component data-centric pipeline for microscale object
detection in industrial environments.
–The Sticky Trap Insect Dataset (STID) dataset family, four progressively
curated versions providing controlled ablation of each pipeline stage.
–Empirical evidence that data engineering alone improves mAP@0.5 from
0.476 to 0.608 on a fixed YOLO baseline (YOLO26-S) [10], demonstrating
that capture standardization supersedes architectural complexity in this do-
main.

2 Related Work
The dominant line of research in pest detection attributes performance limita-
tions to architectural deficiencies and responds by proposing increasingly sophis-
ticated neural networks. The YOLO family remains the most widely adopted
backbone for real-time detection tasks, and several domain-specific variants
have been developed. YOLO-DCPG [6] introduces a lightweight modification of
YOLOv8n aimed at embedded deployment, reducing parameter count by over
50% while improving precision on pest imagery. YOLO-YSTs [7] combines the
NMS-free training strategy of YOLOv10 with SPD-Conv modules to mitigate
information loss in strided convolutions, a critical issue for millimeter-level tar-
gets. VectorBrain [8] extends the task further, concurrently predicting species
identity, sex, and abdomen status from a single forward pass.
While these contributions are technically significant, they share a common
assumption: that the training data is of sufficient quality and consistency to
allow the model to learn. A second body of work attributes performance limi-
tations to data scarcity and attempts to address it through volume expansion.
Synthetic image generation—typically via 3D rendering engines [11], allows the
creation of large annotated datasets without manual collection. Augmentation
pipelines further multiply existing samples through geometric and photometric
transformations [12].
A growing body of work challenges the model-centric consensus by arguing
that, for many tasks, dataset quality is the primary determinant of performance.
Ng [11] formalizes this position under theData-Centric AIparadigm, demon-
strating that systematic dataset improvement yields larger performance gains
than architectural iteration. In the SOD literature, SAHI [9] proposes a data-
pipeline intervention at inference time, slicing images into overlapping patches
to preserve spatial features typically lost in standard convolutions. Research
on edge computing for vision-based insect traps [13] further indicates that un-
standardized datasets are a primary cause of poor field performance, and that
physical controls on image acquisition can significantly improve model reliability.
As Table 1 demonstrates, no existing work applies a complete data-centric
pipeline to microscale industrial pest detection. While current literature pre-
dominantly relies on a model-centric paradigm, our work uniquely integrates a
physical Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) to prevent specimen occlusion
and targeted Noise Mitigation to resolve background clutter. Most importantly,
we validate this pipeline through rigorous data ablation, isolating the impact
of data maturation on a fixed architecture to prove that dataset structural in-
tegrity—not algorithmic complexity, is the true bottleneck in this domain.

3.2 Dataset Curation and Baseline (STID-Clean)
The initial phase of the pipeline utilized STID-Clean, consisting of 500 origi-
nal captures of adhesive traps collected under uncontrolled field conditions. This
baselineallowedforthesystematicidentificationofreal-worldchallengesinherent
to microscale object detection, includingfeature vanishingduring network down-
sampling, severe background clutter caused by industrial residues, and abrupt

scale variations arising from inconsistent camera distances. STID-Clean was an-
notated with five biological classes, namely Fruit Fly (FF), House Fly (HF),
Blow Fly (BF), Flesh Fly (MF), and a generic Other Insects (OI) class covering
specimens that did not fall into the four named taxa, and served as the reference
point against which all subsequent curation stages were evaluated. All images
were captured at a fixed resolution of1,920×1,080pixels and annotated using
Roboflow, with tight axis-aligned bounding boxes drawn to the morphological
limits of each specimen. From STID-Onto onward, a sixth, non-biological class,
Noise, was added to explicitly annotate industrial debris (adhesive dirt, fibres,
and particulate matter) that visually resembles the biological targets, as detailed
in Section 3.4.
3.3 Volume Expansion via Synthetic Data (STID-Raw)
To address data scarcity, a second dataset version, STID-Raw, was produced by
augmenting the original captures with synthetic images generated in Blender.
The 3D modeling pipeline randomized the spatial distribution and angular rota-
tion of virtual insect models, positioning each specimen at the exact focal depth
of the adhesive layer to maximise photorealism. This process yielded over 2,700
training samples. Despite the increased volume, the approach was ultimately
discarded: artificial lighting conditions and the absence of organic adhesive tex-
tures prevented the model from generalizing to real trap images, confirming that
volume alone is insufficient when domain fidelity is low.
3.4 Ontological Refinement (STID-Onto)
Retracting from the synthetic approach, the third iteration refocused on annota-
tionquality.STID-Ontointroducedaformalontologicalrefinementbyexpanding
the label set from five to six classes through the addition of an auxiliaryNoise
class. This class was specifically designed to encode the visual appearance of
industrial debris, adhesive dirt, fibres, and particulate matter, that consistently
triggered false positive detections when morphologically similar to the Fruit Fly.
By explicitly forcing the network to learn whatnotto detect, this intervention
significantly reduced the false positive rate in real operating conditions. Data
augmentation was applied exclusively to the organic captures at this stage to
further improve sample diversity without reintroducing the domain gap observed
in STID-Raw.
3.5 Physical Capture Standardisation (STID-SOP)
The final and most impactful stage of the pipeline addressed the physical bottle-
necks identified in STID-Clean: inconsistent focal distance, variable lighting, and
specimen occlusion due to natural crowding on the adhesive surface. A rigorous
SOPwasestablished,fixingthecamerafocallengthandenforcinguniformillumi-
nation across all acquisitions, thereby guaranteeing a constant spatial resolution

at the millimetre scale. In parallel, structured physical templates, printed grids
placed directly on the adhesive plates—imposed a homogeneous spatial distribu-
tion of specimens, effectively eliminating occlusion and enabling bounding-box
annotations to be adjusted strictly to the morphological limits of each individual.
We refer to the cumulative set of real, non-synthetic captures accumulated
across the STID-Clean and STID-Onto stages as theorganic dataset, as op-
posed to the Blender-generated synthetic images introduced in STID-Raw (Sec-
tion 3.3). STID-SOP is composed exclusively of images captured under these
standardized 2026-protocol plates. Subsequent internal versions within the 5th
iteration series combine these standardized captures with selected legacy im-
ages from the organic dataset, applying class balancing to mitigate the natural
imbalance between abundant and rare species.

4.1 RQ1 — Data Quality versus Model Complexity
Reading Table 4horizontally(across model scales within a fixed dataset) reveals
that scaling from YOLO26-S to YOLO26-X yields negligible and inconsistent im-
provements. On STID-Clean, mAP@0.5 ranges from 0.476 to 0.481, an absolute
spread of only 0.005 points despite a substantial increase in parameter count.
The same plateau appears on STID-Onto (0.381–0.409) and STID-SOP (0.608–
0.534). Across every dataset version, the gain from multiplying model capacity
is marginal and does not follow a consistent upward trend.
Reading the tablevertically(across dataset versions within a fixed model)
reveals the opposite dynamic. Even the smallest variant, YOLO26-S, improved
its mAP@0.5 from 0.476 on STID-Clean to 0.608 on STID-SOP, an absolute
gain of +0.132. This pattern is consistent across all four model sizes, suggesting
that the performance bottleneck resides in the data rather than in the network
architecture.
The explanation lies in the physical nature of the task. When training im-
agescontainoccludedorscale-inconsistentspecimens,themorphologicalfeatures
required for detection are absent or corrupted at the pixel level. No increase
in model capacity can reconstruct information that was never captured at im-
age acquisition. Once physical controls were applied in STID-SOP, the smallest
YOLO26-S architecture learned to detect the same targets that larger models
consistently failed on, confirming that architectural complexity cannot compen-
sate for spatially deficient input.

4.2 RQ2 — Contribution of Each Pipeline Component
The vertical trajectory of Table 4 reveals three qualitatively distinct phases of
dataset evolution, each corresponding to a specific pipeline intervention.
Phase 1: Volume expansion (STID-Raw).The STID-Raw dataset was
constructed by merging the original 500 real captures with more than 2 ,700
syntheticimagesgeneratedbyBlender3Dmodeling,followingthevolume-centric
hypothesis that more training data would benefit the model. The validation
metrics for STID-Raw, to be fully reported upon completion of current training
runs, are expected to confirm an apparent improvement over STID-Clean on the
held-out validation split.
However, this apparent gain is accompanied by a qualitative failure mode ob-
served during visual inspection of predictions on genuine adhesive plates outside
the training distribution: models trained on STID-Raw exhibited a higher rate of
false negatives on real specimens and false positives on adhesive residue, consis-
tent with a domain gap between the synthetic and organic image distributions.
Synthetic images, despite correct geometric placement, lack the organic surface
textures of real insect bodies and the characteristic appearance of adhesive sub-
strates under authentic illumination conditions. A quantitative evaluation on
held-out real-world deployment data is left for future work.
This phenomenon illustrates a fundamental limitation of volume-centric ap-
proaches: validation-set performance computed on in-distribution data (where
synthetic and real are mixed) can be a misleading proxy for real-world relia-
bility when the training distribution diverges from the deployment distribution.
Notably, STID-SOP relies exclusively on real captures under standardized con-
ditions, which by construction avoids this synthetic-to-real domain gap.
This finding demonstrates that, for microscale biological targets with distinc-
tive organic textures, synthetic data augmentation not only fails to generalize
but actively misleads evaluation, creating a false impression of progress that
disappears under real operational conditions.
Phase 2: Ontological refinement (STID-Onto).Recognizing that tex-
ture fidelity alone was insufficient, STID-Onto introduced an ontological inter-
vention: the addition of an auxiliaryNoiseclass comprising annotated industrial
debris and adhesive residue that visually mimics millimeter-sized insects. This
forced the network to explicitly learn whatnotto detect, providing direct su-
pervision over the primary source of false positives. The resulting mAP@0.5 for
YOLO26-S reached 0.381, lower in absolute terms than STID-Clean, but accom-
panied by substantially higher Precision (0.574 versus 0.423), indicating that the
model began making fewer false biological detections.
Phase 3: Physical capture standardization (STID-SOP).The decisive
intervention was the implementation of a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP)
thatfixedfocallengthandstandardizedillumination,combinedwithprintedgrid
templates on the adhesive plates to enforce specimen separation. This addressed
the two physical bottlenecks that ontological refinement alone could not solve:
inter-image scale variance and bounding-box overlap from specimen crowding.
The transition from STID-Onto to STID-SOP produced the largest single gain

4.3 RQ3 — Industrial Viability of a Standard Model
The third research question asks whether an architecturally unmodified model,
trained on a rigorously curated dataset, can achieve detection performance com-
parable to that reported by specialized architectures in the literature.
Theresultsconfirmthishypothesis.YOLO26-StrainedonSTID-SOPachieved
a global mAP@0.5 of 0.608, with Precision of 0.527 and Recall of 0.652. These
figures are directly comparable to those reported by architecturally customized
models such as YOLO-DCPG [6] and YOLO-YSTs [7], which operate on less
challenging, better-controlled datasets and rely on structural modifications to
achieve their gains. Our baseline model, without any such modifications, reaches
equivalent performance through data quality alone. Notably, on our own dataset
this relationship inverts once the data-centric pipeline is applied: on STID-Clean,
YOLO26-L holds a marginal edge over YOLO26-S (0.482 versus 0.476), whereas
on STID-SOP YOLO26-S (0.608) clearly outperforms YOLO26-L (0.547). This
reversal is direct evidence that dataset quality, not model capacity, is the domi-
nant factor governing detection performance in this task.
Furthermore, considering the benchmark established by manual inspection
(the Technique for Human Error Rate Prediction, THERP, estimates human
error rates of up to 30% in repetitive visual tasks [4]), a Recall of 0.652 on an
unconstrained microscale dataset represents a performance level that is indus-
trially competitive. As physical standardisation is progressively extended to the
full operational environment, these metrics are expected to improve further.
The residual classification errors provide additional evidence that the remain-
inglimitationsarephysicalratherthanalgorithmic.TheconfusionbetweenFlesh
Fly (MF) and Blow Fly (BF) reflects genuine morphological similarity that chal-
lenges even trained human taxonomists. The misclassification between Fruit Fly
(FF) and theNoiseclass occurs because sub-millimeter debris is physically in-
distinguishable from the insect’s body under standard imaging conditions. These
are not model failures; they are the irreducible boundaries of the detection task
itself.
5 Conclusion
This work demonstrates that, for the task of microscale object detection in in-
dustrial pest control, the structural integrity of the training dataset is the pri-
mary determinant of model performance, fundamentally superseding architec-
tural complexity. Through a controlled ablation across four progressively curated
versions of the STID dataset and four YOLO26 model scales, we showed that

scaling model capacity within a fixed dataset yields negligible gains, whereas sys-
tematic data engineering, comprising ontological refinement through the intro-
duction of aNoiseclass and physical capture standardisation via a printed-grid
SOP and fixed focal distance, produced the decisive performance gains observed.
Theresidualclassificationerrorsinthefinalmodel,suchastheconfusionbetween
morphologically similar species, reflect the taxonomic boundaries of the target
species rather than algorithmic deficiencies, suggesting that scaling the pipeline
to a larger number of visually similar species will likely require finer-grained mor-
phological features rather than dataset volume alone. Dense crowding scenarios
beyond what the printed-grid SOP resolves may also benefit from instance seg-
mentation, though this was not explored here given its added annotation cost.
Future work will focus on deploying the model to edge devices for real-time field
monitoring, validating the pipeline across multiple random seeds, and investigat-
ing self-supervised learning techniques to reduce the manual annotation effort
required for emerging pest species.
